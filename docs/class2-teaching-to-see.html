Class 2: Review of Teaching to See

Notes on Inge Druckrey’s teaching, and what it implies for charts.

Film: Teaching to See, directed by Andrei Severny, produced by Edward Tufte, on the teaching of designer Inge Druckrey (37 min).

Opening impression

The image that stayed with me was the Beethoven poster, where the letter E is drawn together with the lines of a musical staff. I did not see the staff at first; it appeared only after I had been looking for a while. That is the point of the film in one image: visual design is not decoration handed to the viewer all at once. Some things have to be looked at for a while before the details come out. Druckrey trains exactly this kind of looking.

1. Thin/thick and curve/linearity

The exercises focus on the thick and thin parts of a letter. At first I did not understand why a stroke would be thicker at the ends and thinner in the middle, but the result looks balanced and calm rather than stiff. The variation comes from the writing tool: a broad pen held at a fixed angle produces a wide mark in one direction and a narrow one in the other, so the thickness of a curve changes continuously as it turns. Where a straight stem meets a curve, that transition has to be smoothed by hand, or the letter looks broken. Redrawing the same letter many times is how the students learn to feel where the weight should go.

The work is also about space, not only about the strokes themselves. The Roman alphabet is remarkably symmetrical, and when the students modify an existing typeface, every letter has to be adjusted so that the spaces inside and between the letters stay even. Design has to leave room.

2. The eye and the hand

Holmes separates writing into two opposing aspects. The eye is the conservative one: “the eye wants to look at an orderly set of marks on a page — the eye wants pattern,” something regular and reliable. The hand is “the radical aspect of writing”: it wants to move, and to “write faster and faster.” Writing is the negotiation between them.

A signature is where that tension becomes visible. It is produced too quickly for the eye to impose order on it, so what remains is the trace of the hand — which is exactly why a signature is unique and hard to forget. The letterform exercises in the film work in the opposite direction: they slow the hand down until the eye can lead.

3. Calligraphy, Steve Jobs, and the Macintosh

Jobs took a calligraphy class, and the film connects that class to the typography of the Macintosh. What he learned was not only that letters can be beautiful. Calligraphy teaches judgment about proportion, spacing, and the small visual corrections that make a line of text read well. When the Macintosh shipped with proportionally spaced fonts and careful letterforms, it carried that judgment into a machine, and from there into everything that was made with it.

4. Geometric accuracy versus optical accuracy

Geometric accuracy and optical accuracy are not the same thing. A letter can be measured and constructed so that every dimension is exactly equal, and it will still look wrong to the eye — a little heavy here, a little short there. The eye does not measure; it compares, and it is easily misled by shape. So the designer deliberately introduces small deviations from the geometry until the letter looks right, and at that point the viewer accepts it as correct without noticing that anything was changed.

The thinning of a stem toward its middle, which I described above, is one of these corrections: geometrically the stroke is no longer of even width, but optically it reads as straighter and steadier than a stroke of constant width.

The same judgment governs the alphabet as a whole. In the film the students work toward three things at once. First, the letters have to share a common structure, so that they read as one alphabet rather than a collection of separate drawings. Second, each letter has to stay different enough from every other letter to remain legible — the shared structure cannot be pushed so far that an a starts to look like an o. Third, the spacing between letters has to produce an even rhythm and an even color on the page.

Spacing is the clearest case of optical over geometric accuracy. Equal measured gaps do not look equal, because the shapes on either side differ: the space between two straight stems and the space beside a round or diagonal letter are measured the same and seen differently. The spacing is therefore set by eye until the line of text has an even texture, not by making the distances equal.

This is why Druckrey’s students correct their letters by eye rather than by ruler. Being measurably right is not the goal; being read correctly is.

What this means for data visualization

Druckrey’s students spend hours on one letter, redrawing it and adjusting the space around it. That is the same work we do when we choose an axis range, a color, or how much white space a chart gets. In both cases the measurement is not the goal — being read correctly is. A chart can be geometrically accurate, with every value plotted exactly, and still be read wrongly, in the same way that a geometrically perfect letter can look wrong. Anscombe’s quartet is the statistical version of the same lesson, and the bubble chart I critiqued in Assignment 1 is the visual one: the circles were scaled correctly by area, yet readers still could not see how much the banks had lost.

Learning to see is learning to check what the reader’s eye actually receives, not what the number says should be there.
