Class 5: More Data, Better Questions

Google Flu Trends, overfitting, and Wickham’s visualization workflow

Published

October 7, 2026

a. Big data analytics pitfalls

Google Flu Trends (GFT) offered a compelling shortcut: estimate influenza-like illness from searches before conventional surveillance arrived, with roughly a one-day lag (Ginsberg et al., 2009). But searches measure information-seeking, not infection, and the CDC target itself measures the share of visits involving influenza-like symptoms, not laboratory-confirmed influenza.

Big data hubris is the assumption that abundant digital traces can replace established measurement rather than supplement it. Lazer et al. (2014) report that GFT overestimated the CDC measure in 100 of 108 weeks from August 2011. More searches did not repair the gap between searching and being ill.

Algorithm dynamics make that gap unstable. Search suggestions and symptom prompts change what people type without any change in illness; Lazer and colleagues treat these platform changes as plausible contributors rather than a proven single cause. A survey instrument does not revise itself; a commercial search engine does, for reasons unrelated to flu.

Transparency and replicability are why the failure took years to diagnose. The 45 search terms behind the model were never published, and Google Correlate, the public tool released afterwards, does not return the example terms given in the GFT papers, so the result cannot be reconstructed from outside.

b. Overfitting and overparameterization

Overfitting occurs when a procedure captures accidental features of its training sample and so generalizes poorly. Overparameterization describes a model with more adjustable parameters than the observations can constrain. The first is a failure of performance, the second a condition of capacity: regularization can keep a heavily parameterized model from generalizing badly.

The danger in GFT lived in feature selection rather than in the model. The procedure screened about 50 million candidate queries against 1,152 weekly observations and summed the best 45 into a single predictor; the final regression then carried an intercept and a slope, not 50 million coefficients (Ginsberg et al., 2009). Counting those two coefficients misses the flexibility spent on the search, and a search that wide will find coincidences — Lazer et al. note seasonal candidates such as high-school basketball.

I have met the same structure in my own work. Trying to improve a medical-imaging classifier, I concatenated features from several deep learning backbones, expecting the ensemble to beat any single model; training performance was excellent and test performance got worse. The feature count grew with every backbone I added, the number of labelled images did not, and choosing which backbones to keep by their held-out scores spent further flexibility that never appears in the final classifier.

c. Wickham talk summary

In his EMBL keynote, Wickham frames visualization as one stage of an iterative workflow — import, tidy, transform, visualise, model, communicate — not as the step that produces a finished picture. Two demonstrations made that concrete. A few lines of gganimate turned a static income-and-life-expectancy chart into a draggable time axis, rendering slowly enough that he joked about the frame rate. He then knitted one R Markdown file holding both the prose and the code that fetched the data, so the figures arrived already computed: nothing is pasted in by hand, nothing goes stale when the data change.

His central distinction is between exploratory and expositional graphics. Exploratory plots are for the analyst who owns the data, and the only way to make good ones is to make a great many bad ones (20:08). A plot also says what to do next: a mass of overplotted points is itself evidence of structure the model has not accounted for.

Where I would qualify him is the place of tidy data. Tidying first works when the data are a table of observations and variables. The data I work with are image tensors: the natural representation is a dense array, the operations that matter are optimised linear algebra, and a long format would store a row key and a column key for every single value, at a cost in memory before any analysis begins. Tidy data is a sound principle about what counts as one observation, but as a compulsory first stage it assumes a shape of data my field does not have.
